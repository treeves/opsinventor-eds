AEM Infrastructure and Personnel Strategy: A Talk with Tom Johnson of Hirobe

OI
OpsInventor Editorial
May 2024 · 12 min read
Key topics
Cloud vs on-premise AEM Staffing strategy Migration decision factors AEMaaCS readiness

Tom Johnson leads infrastructure and delivery at Hirobe, a professional services firm that has helped organizations across enterprise sectors navigate AEM deployments for over a decade. We sat down with him to discuss what it actually takes to move from on-premise or AMS AEM to Adobe's cloud-native offering — and what most organizations get wrong before they start.

"The technical migration is honestly the easier part. Where teams struggle is the organizational readiness — who owns what, how decisions get made, and whether the people in the room actually have authority to commit."

— Tom Johnson, Hirobe

The Cloud vs. On-Premise Decision

Many organizations still run AEM on-premise or through Adobe Managed Services and face real pressure to evaluate AEM as a Cloud Service. The promise is continuous updates, reduced ops burden, and tighter Adobe product integration. But the tradeoffs are real.

Q
When you're advising a client about whether to move to AEM as a Cloud Service, what's the first question you ask?
Tom
I ask them how much custom code they're running. Not just the volume — the kind. If they have a lot of tightly coupled integrations, custom OSGi services with complex lifecycle dependencies, or heavy use of deprecated APIs, the migration story gets complicated fast. That doesn't mean don't go — it means the prep work is bigger than they think.
Q
What about cost? Cloud is often sold as a cost-saving move.
Tom
It can be. But organizations often undercount the transition costs — the engineering time to refactor code, the learning curve for teams unfamiliar with cloud-native AEM patterns, and the potential need to rethink how content is delivered. The ongoing licensing model is different too. You're paying for a managed service, not a perpetual license. That's not bad — but you need to plan for it honestly.

Staffing and Team Structure

One of the underreported challenges in AEM cloud migrations is organizational: who knows what, who owns the new environment, and how do you build institutional knowledge as the platform evolves under continuous delivery?

Q
How do you advise clients on staffing for a migration?
Tom
You need someone — ideally more than one person — who deeply understands both the existing implementation and the target state. That's rare. Most teams have either people who know the current system intimately but haven't worked cloud-native, or consultants who know AEMaaCS patterns but don't know the client's specific implementation quirks. The overlap is hard to find. We usually recommend building a small tiger team of internal engineers who get deep training on the cloud environment before the migration work begins in earnest.

"Don't outsource your architectural understanding. You can outsource execution, but someone on your team needs to own the 'why' of every decision, or you'll be dependent on external support forever."

— Tom Johnson, Hirobe

What Actually Goes Wrong

We asked Tom to be direct about the failure modes he sees most often. His answer was unambiguous.

Tom
In order: underestimating the BPA findings, starting the migration before the content strategy is defined, and assuming the Dispatcher config from on-premise will translate cleanly. It almost never does. The cache invalidation model is different, the delivery tier behaves differently — teams that just lift and shift their vhost and dispatcher config are in for a rough go-live.

The Bottom Line

Moving to AEM as a Cloud Service is the right direction for most organizations that are heavily invested in the Adobe ecosystem. But it's a genuine migration — not an upgrade — and it deserves a genuine migration plan: inventory of custom code, organizational readiness assessment, a realistic timeline, and people who understand what they're building toward, not just moving away from.
